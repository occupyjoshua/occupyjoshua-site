Death.

Apr 28, 2026

Several months ago I buried my grandfather. I know it’s a strange way to introduce a topic, but it felt fitting for a discussion on death. I preached a sermon at his funeral on the value of hope, but it struck me throughout the planning and lead up, how little we in the west actually come face to face with death, and how absurdly abnormal that is (historically speaking). My cousin remarked that she had never seen a casket in person. Early last year I did a 12 week intensive practicum working as an on-call chaplain for a major hospital. I was flung from the comfortable, naïve, western world, into one of the only places in our society that actually still confronts death head on, and it was an interesting experience to say the least. It’s hard to oversell just how averse to death we are as a society, but it’s no surprise really. Death is the ultimate absolute that spits in the face of a culture that pretends that absolutes don’t actually exist. Truth is subjective and reality is malleable, until death comes knocking.

I operate from a Christ-centered worldview, so I imagine that my experiences and perspectives on death may strike some of you as odd. Death, for the Christian, is many things at once. It’s the culmination of a life lived, it’s a metaphor for how we are to live that life, it’s an end, and a beginning. It’s a gateway into a better tomorrow, and yet death itself is not to be desired, so much as what naturally follows it. That which lies on the other side. It represents an opportunity to join with the church throughout history, and to experience our relationship with God in a whole new way. Despite the generally positive nature of most of these attributes I have listed, I don’t want anyone to think that it lacks gravity, weight, and sadness. These things of course are still expected and natural, and certainly very familiar for me. Lamentations is the name of one of the books of the Bible, and it is essentially a book of mourning and grieving death, and suffering.

The culture at large has a very low view of death, probably because we’ve all become so desensitized to it (in certain contexts, but not in others). It’s strange to think about really; how many of you reading have ever seen a dead body in real life? Touched one? And yet, how many of us have watched horrible deaths actually occur in front of our eyes through some sort of screen? When Kirk was assassinated, how many people actually watched it happen on socials? We distance ourselves and become numb to the intensity of it, while also avoiding any actual confrontation with the reality of it. Backwards world.

I say all this because in many ways I view the world through the lens of tension and release; without tension, the release or resolution loses its meaning and importance. I think we have done the same thing with death and life. Because we cheapen the impact of death, we invariably cheapen the impact of life.

I remember early last year I had the privilege of attending the graduation of a group therapy sort of ministry at our church and I listened to the story of a mother (who is well known to me) talk about what brought her to that place. Her family had been through some pretty brutal suffering over the past few years, some of which I had witnessed and supported her son and daughter through. She talked about how the death of her young niece tore their family apart, and how she thought that would be the end of it, but somehow bad things just kept happening as the years wore on. She talked about how this all broke her down and required a choice from her; despair or God. This public declaration, after a long year of work, highlighted how beautiful life can be if we learn to accept our suffering. If we learn to make peace with death, something astounding happens. The tension in her life was resolved when she turned back towards her savior.

As I process my thoughts on death and write this, I am reminded of one of my favorite scenes in the Marvel Cinematic Universe; the scene between Vision and the final Ultron body. Ultron is about to die, and they have a short discussion on death and life and their respective meanings, and Vision says something profound; ‘a thing isn’t beautiful because it lasts’. A flower fades, a bird dies, one of the defining characteristics of life is that it changes, it moves, it pulls and pushes, and part of the reason why we assign the term ‘beautiful’ to it, is because of that fact. The fact that life cannot stay where it is.

To be frank, I do not know how to offer hope to those outside Christ. I personally have found no other rational source for optimism in this life other than the eternal security that is afforded to me as a result of submission to Jesus Christ. It is the reason I can look at the world and not be crushed by the weight of suffering and death. I do know, however, that viewing death in its proper place, as something which lends meaning to life, something which acts as the foil to life, is the best way of thinking about it that I have found.
