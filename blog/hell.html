Hell.

Jan 12, 2026

Last week we discussed the definition of good, which I put forth as the harmony between action, purpose, and potential. Those actions which most properly align us with our purpose and potential (as defined by God, of course) are good. This week I wish to run the thought experiment in the opposite direction, and to its natural (or perhaps unnatural) end. There may be some truth to the idea that we damn ourselves to hell, and that in so doing, we desecrate the one thing inside ourselves that makes us truly human.

What happens when we consistently and defiantly choose actions which oppose our potential? What are the consequences of such decisions? Well if I had to use one word, I would use the word hell. This may be for reasons that you do not expect however. Let me be clear, regarding hell, there is a punitive, retributive aspect. In a technical sense, the Bible teaches that God created hell for the purpose of imprisoning Satan and his fallen angels, and that it essentially serves a dual purpose as the place for humans who reject God to go.

That being said, today I wish not to focus on the legal/penitentiary aspects of hell, but rather the state which accompanies them. In other words, what characterizes this experience, and what leads to it? If a human being, designed in the very image of God (imago dei), were to use his free will to openly, repeatedly, and ultimately defy the one who made him, what would happen? C.S. Lewis poses an answer to this question in his work ‘The Problem of Pain’’;

“To enter heaven is to become more human than you ever succeeded in being in earth; to enter hell, is to be banished from humanity. What is cast (or casts itself) into hell is not a man: it is “remains”.” - C.S. Lewis1

What Lewis is getting at here is the idea that at the very core of human existence is the image of God. To forsake that image is to forsake being human. It is what we would call ‘subhuman’. The implication here is that human beings, by virtue of being rational, free agents, have the ability to reject that which we were made for. We are unique in this way, we can choose to sin, to reject our telos. The consequences of sin are not merely temporal, however, in that they invariably prevent us from properly fulfilling our potential as image bearers. As Lewis points out, hell is the residence of such ‘sub humans’, or as he calls them ‘remains’, a place for people who are a mere shadow of their former selves.

This aspect of hell, where image bearers of God desecrate the very thing that makes them human, therefore becoming something less than, is often overlooked. I believe this constitutes what the Bible calls ‘eternal death’ or ‘eternal punishment’, not an annihilationist ceasing to exist, but rather a destruction of the one thing that makes us recognizably human. The entity in question is not removed from existence per se, but rather devolved beyond recognition. This disfigurement results in someone who is no longer recognizably human, someone who has lost/destroyed the very fundamental image that they were created to bear. After all, how could one eternally reject God, but remain an image bearer of His?

In this vein, we can understand Hell as something akin to eternal conscious torment, in that it is eternal, conscious, and full of torment, but that those three are actually symptoms of one’s loss of self, and the subsequent mutilation of the image of God(C.S. Lewis’ “The Great Divorce” explores this idea more).

So how does one get to this point? I have come to understand the justification for hell by understanding the concept of recidivism. Recidivism is essentially just the relapse of criminal behavior by someone who has already served their sentence. My concern however, is an eternal focus on recidivism. It is often argued that God cannot be just, and still damn people to eternal hell for temporal sin. In other words, how could any eternal punishment be justified on the basis of non-eternal actions? The short answer is that I don’t think we are damned on the basis of merely temporal actions. I think God allows us to make our own choices, and that He has mercy on those who repent. I also think that there are some who will not repent, and instead simply keep re-offending. They will relapse into sin, they will do so without remorse, and without regard for the God who made them. They will eternally reject Him, perpetually sin, and in this sense, they are not in Hell for temporal actions, but rather for eternal, perpetually reoccurring ones. They are victims, not of an all powerful immoral monster, but rather of their own devices. This is what I call ‘Recidivistic Damnation’.

Another important thing to note; if this is true, then human beings are at least partially responsible. It’s still proper to talk about the role of God’s sovereignty with regards to hell, but this means that each of us individually has a role to play in our own eternal status. Exactly to what degree this is the case, remains a mystery (and perhaps a video for another day).

When we reject God, and His good and perfect will for our lives, we are doing something consequential. This is not just another choice we make among many, it has very real and serious implications. We are rejecting the very source of our being, and the right and proper intention behind it. It is to reject our humanity in favour of something less than, and doing so has consequences.

C.S. Lewis is famously quoted as saying ‘the doors of Hell are locked on the inside’, and he consistently poses this idea in his books on the topic. For C.S Lewis, the idea that humans are their own captors, is instrumental to understanding hell. I agree, but this distinction is not either or. Rather I believe that both human will, and God’s wrath poured out, work in conjunction to produce what we refer to as hell. Romans 1 is rather instrumental to understanding how humans reject God, stating essentially that they choose to live in rebellion, and eventually God exercises His wrath on the unrighteous by allowing them to wallow in the ‘due penalty for their error’.

This is the terrifying reality of Hell; humans choose it, God allows us to choose it, and ultimately it results in a loss of whatever connection to God we have, as well as the very image that makes us human.

1

Lewis, C. S.. (The Problem of Pain.)
