The Art of Christian Rage Bait

Oct 27, 2026

Prophetic speech in an age of outrage.

Over the past six months or so I have been tinkering with a paradox: how do Christians faithfully engage on social media platforms in a way that both honors Christ and succeeds algorithmically?

Social media algorithms are optimized to keep the most amount of people on their platforms for the longest amount of time. Outrage is the most common way today to reach that goal. This stands in obvious contrast to Christian virtue, since such content relies so heavily on a lack of charity, and/or humility, and/or love.

In my mind, however, there must be a way to harmonize Christian orthopraxy with growth and reach on social media. I think we might find that balance by looking at the Old Testament prophets.

In ancient Israel, prophets served as a wake-up call to a nation who refused to acknowledge God, no matter how many times He proved Himself to them. God used the prophets to speak truth to a society that abandoned Him over and over again. In their example we can find some truths that answer my question.

They routinely spoke out boldly against evil in the public square (Amos 5:10). They routinely called the people to repentance (Isaiah 1:16–18). As a result, they were often scapegoated and turned into pariahs (Jeremiah 20:7–10).

In the NT, John the Baptist continued this tradition of boldly proclaiming truth in the face of the cultural powers of the day (Matthew 3). Christ Himself also did so on plenty of occasions, and ultimately taught that those of us who follow Christ will draw the ire of those who hate Him (John 15:18–21).

How many cultural beliefs can you think of that might fit that category today? How many examples of blatant and unrepentant evil do you regularly encounter on X? I see it every day: public support of evil in ways none of us would prefer to think about.

Christians ought not to cause evil, nor should they promote or participate in it. Public rebuke is none of those things. It is the proper response to the public exercise of evil, and in the tradition of the prophets, I believe it contains the answer to my question.

Christ naturally offends the conscience of the unregenerate. Any bold attempt to reclaim the public square for His glory will be met with fervent opposition, which makes our content the very definition of rage bait.

In the same way that the prophets of old garnered notoriety by simply pointing at an evil thing and calling it evil, we can gain infamy.

I remember years ago when Jordan Peterson was being interviewed by Joe Rogan, he stated something rather funny: “I have figured out how to monetize social justice warriors.” The idea here is similar in some ways. The very act of calling people to repentance will draw their attention. That attention will be largely negative because the world, the flesh, and the devil are all adamantly opposed to it. They will be unable to restrain their ire, which is quite helpful for growing a social media account.

It is important to note, however, that rage bait cannot become the goal. Christ must be our first priority, not infamy, and it will be easy to confuse the two when most Christian doctrine offends the conscience. Tearing down idols is good. Farming engagement for its own sake is not.

The risk remains on these platforms. We should never forget to practice in a way that Christ Himself would approve of, balancing grace and truth (John 1:14). Not every post needs to go viral. Not every discussion is fitting at every moment. There is a time and a season for everything under the sun, and it is our job as Christians to faithfully discern the propriety of a given act at a given time.

Tread carefully. Speak truth in love. Extend grace to those who need it. Rebuke those who need it. Do so boldly and apologetically, bounded by the Spirit of God, and watch the demons screech.
